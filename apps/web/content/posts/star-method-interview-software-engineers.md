You can reverse a binary tree on a whiteboard and still lose the offer in the behavioural round. It happens constantly, and it is almost never because the candidate had nothing to say. It is because they told a rambling story with no structure, no clear personal contribution, and no ending.

The STAR method fixes that. It is a four-part structure - Situation, Task, Action, Result - for answering any question that starts with "tell me about a time when." It is not a script, and interviewers are not scoring you on whether you used it. They are scoring you on whether they could follow what you did and why it mattered, and STAR is simply the most reliable way to make that happen under pressure.

This guide covers what each part actually means for engineers, how long to spend on each, full worked answers, and the specific mistakes that make otherwise strong stories fall flat.

## What STAR Actually Stands For

**Situation** - the context. Where you were, what the team was, what was going on. Two sentences.

**Task** - what you specifically were responsible for. Not what the team's goal was: what landed on you. One or two sentences.

**Action** - what you did. This is the answer. Specific decisions, specific trade-offs, specific things you built or changed.

**Result** - what happened. Numbers where you have them, and what you learned or changed afterwards.

The rough time budget matters as much as the structure:

| Part | Share of the answer | Roughly |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | 10% | 10-15 seconds |
| Task | 10% | 10-15 seconds |
| Action | 60% | 60-70 seconds |
| Result | 20% | 20-25 seconds |

Almost every weak answer gets this ratio wrong in the same direction: two minutes of context, twenty seconds of what they actually did, and no result at all. The [Tech Interview Handbook's behavioural guide](https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org/behavioral-interview/) makes the same point from a different angle - the interviewer needs signal about *you*, and context is not signal.

## Why Interviewers Use Behavioural Questions At All

It is worth understanding what is being measured, because it changes what you emphasise.

Companies with published hiring rubrics are fairly open about this. [Google describes its interview process](https://careers.google.com/how-we-hire/interview/) as looking for how you have handled situations in the past as a predictor of how you will handle them in future. Amazon takes it furthest: its behavioural loop maps almost every question directly onto one of its [16 Leadership Principles](https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles), and interviewers are literally scoring against a named principle.

The practical implication: behavioural questions are not vibes. Each one is a probe for a specific trait - ownership, dealing with ambiguity, disagreeing well, handling failure. Your job is to give evidence for the trait being probed.

## The Four Parts, For Engineers

### Situation: be boring and be fast

The interviewer needs just enough to understand the stakes. Team size, what the system did, what was going wrong.

> "I was the second engineer on a four-person team owning our billing service. Invoices were generated by a nightly cron job, and around a quarter of them were arriving late enough that support was fielding complaints every morning."

That is it. Do not explain your company's org structure. Do not explain what a cron job is.

### Task: make your scope unambiguous

This is the part engineers skip most often, and skipping it costs you. Without it, the interviewer cannot tell which parts of the Action were yours.

> "I owned the fix end to end - diagnosis, implementation and rollout. My manager gave me two weeks and asked me not to take the service down to do it."

Constraints belong here. "Two weeks, no downtime" tells the interviewer more about the difficulty than any amount of Situation would.

### Action: the actual answer

This is where 60% of your words go, and where most candidates go abstract. Compare:

> Weak: "I looked into the problem, worked with the team, and we optimised the job."

> Strong: "I started by adding timing instrumentation around each stage of the job, because we had no visibility into where the time was going. That showed 80% of the runtime was a single N+1 query pattern - we were fetching line items per invoice inside a loop over 40,000 invoices. I rewrote it as a single batched query with a join, which took the stage from 26 minutes to about 90 seconds. I did not touch the rest of the pipeline, even though I could see other slow parts, because the deadline was tight and this was the dominant cost. I put it behind a feature flag, ran both paths in parallel for two nights, and diffed the output before removing the old path."

The strong version answers questions the interviewer has not asked yet: how did you find the problem, why that fix, what did you deliberately *not* do, how did you de-risk the change. That is what "senior" sounds like.

Two rules for this section:

**Say "I", not "we".** You will feel like you are stealing credit. You are not - the interviewer already assumes you had a team. When you say "we decided to batch the query," you have told them nothing about yourself. Use "we" for context and "I" for actions.

**Include one decision you got wrong or reconsidered.** Candidates who present a flawless linear path read as either lucky or unreliable narrators. "My first attempt was to parallelise the loop, which made the database contention worse - I backed that out after a day" makes the whole story more credible, not less.

### Result: never skip this

The single most common failure in behavioural answers is an answer that just stops. The interviewer has to prompt: "and how did that go?" - and you have burned the impression.

Quantify when you can. Deploy time, error rate, latency, hours saved per week, tickets avoided, percentage of a build. If you genuinely have no numbers, use a concrete qualitative outcome instead:

> "Late invoices went from about 25% to zero over the following month, and support stopped getting the morning complaints entirely. I wrote up the N+1 pattern in our engineering wiki because I found two other jobs doing the same thing, and we fixed both the following quarter."

The final sentence is doing extra work: it shows impact beyond the immediate task, which is exactly the signal that separates a mid-level answer from a senior one.

## Three Full Worked Answers

### "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate."

**Situation.** "On my last team, a senior engineer proposed moving our background jobs onto a new queue system he had used before. We had four services depending on the existing one."

**Task.** "I was the main maintainer of two of those services, so I was going to do most of the migration work and own the fallout if it went badly."

**Action.** "I did not want to argue from gut feeling, so I spent a day writing up what the migration would actually cost - about three weeks of my time, plus a rewrite of our retry logic, which was the part I was most worried about. Then I listed what we would get: better observability, and a managed service instead of one we ran ourselves. Both real. I took it to him directly before raising it in the team channel, because I did not want it to feel like an ambush. We went through my estimate together and he pushed back on two of my numbers, fairly - I had over-estimated the retry work. Where we landed was that the benefits were real but not urgent, and we agreed to migrate one low-risk service first as a trial rather than all four at once."

**Result.** "The trial migration took nine days and surfaced a serialisation issue we would have hit across all four services simultaneously if we had done a big-bang cutover. We finished the rest the following quarter. The thing I took from it was that writing the cost down turned a disagreement about opinions into a conversation about numbers, and that fixed most of the friction on its own."

### "Tell me about a time you failed."

Pick something with real consequences. A story where the failure is "I was too much of a perfectionist" wastes everyone's time.

**Situation.** "I shipped a database migration that took our main product read path down for about forty minutes during business hours."

**Task.** "I had written the migration and I was the one who ran it."

**Action.** "I had tested it against a staging database, but staging had about 50,000 rows and production had 12 million. The migration added a non-null column with a default, which on our Postgres version rewrote the entire table and held a lock the whole time. When reads started timing out I did not immediately connect it to the migration - I spent the first ten minutes looking at the application layer, which made it worse. Once I realised, I could not safely cancel it partway, so the correct call was to let it finish rather than risk a partial state. I stayed on the incident call and gave support a running estimate so they could tell customers something true."

**Result.** "Forty-one minutes of degraded reads. Afterwards I wrote the postmortem myself. Two things changed: we started requiring migrations to be tested against a production-sized snapshot, and I added a checklist item for locking behaviour that I still use. I have run a lot of migrations since and I have never again run one without knowing what lock it takes."

Note what makes this work: real consequences, no blaming staging or the team, honest about the ten minutes he wasted looking in the wrong place, and a concrete process change at the end.

### "Tell me about a time you had to work with an ambiguous requirement."

**Situation.** "Product asked for 'a way for admins to see what users are doing.' That was the entire brief."

**Task.** "I was the only engineer on it, with a two-sprint budget."

**Action.** "Rather than guess, I asked to sit with two of the support leads for an hour each. What became obvious was that they did not want an activity feed at all - what they actually did twenty times a day was try to reconstruct why one specific user had ended up in a broken state. So I scoped it down to a single-user timeline view with the eight event types they mentioned, and deliberately left out the dashboard and the aggregate reporting that the original brief implied. I wrote that scope down in a one-page doc and got product to confirm it before I wrote any code, because I knew I was cutting a lot."

**Result.** "It shipped in one sprint instead of two. Support's average time to diagnose an account issue dropped from around fifteen minutes to under two, which they measured themselves. The aggregate dashboard never got built, and nobody asked for it, which I think validated the cut."

## The Six Stories That Cover Almost Everything

You cannot prepare an answer per question - there are hundreds of question variants. You can prepare six stories that map onto nearly all of them:

1. **A hard technical problem you solved** - depth, debugging, judgement
2. **A conflict or disagreement** - collaboration, influence without authority
3. **A failure with real consequences** - self-awareness, accountability
4. **Something you led or drove** - ownership, initiative
5. **A tight deadline or trade-off you had to make** - prioritisation, pragmatism
6. **An ambiguous problem you scoped yourself** - dealing with uncertainty

Write each one out in full once. Not to memorise - to find out where the gaps are. You will discover you cannot remember the numbers for story three and that story five is actually the same story as story one. Better to find that at your desk than in the interview.

Then practise saying them out loud. Reading a story and telling a story are different skills, and only one of them is being tested. ## Mistakes That Sink Good Stories

**Starting too early.** "So I joined the company in 2022, and at the time the team was..." Nobody needs the prehistory. Start where the problem starts.

**Hiding behind "we".** If your entire Action section is in the first-person plural, the interviewer has learned nothing about you.

**No result.** Covered above, and worth repeating because it is the number one issue.

**Picking a story with no stakes.** "A time I showed leadership" answered with a university group project, when you have three years of industry experience, reads as a failure to search your own memory.

**Being unable to go one level deeper.** The follow-up is always coming. "Why did you choose a join over a subquery there?" If your story is memorised rather than remembered, this is where it falls apart. This is precisely why you prepare stories, not scripts.

**Answering a different question.** Interviewers ask about conflict and get a story about a hard bug. If you have to bend a story more than slightly to fit, use a different story - or say "the closest thing I have is..." and be upfront about it.

## Practising Properly

Reading this article will not change your performance. Three things will:

1. **Write the six stories out.** Longhand, once. Find the missing numbers and go look them up in old tickets or commit history if you have to.
2. **Record yourself answering.** Your phone is enough. You will immediately hear the filler, the rambling Situation, and the missing Result. Almost every fixable weakness is obvious on playback and invisible in the moment.
3. **Get follow-up questions.** The value of a mock is not the question, it is the "why did you do it that way?" afterwards. Practise with a peer, or use AI mocks for volume and consistency - the point is that something pushes back.

Behavioural rounds are the most improvable part of the entire interview process. The material is fixed - you already lived it. All you are doing is learning to tell it in ninety seconds with the important parts in front.
